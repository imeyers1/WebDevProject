Rock Climbing

One of my favorite hobbies — and a guide to get you started

Why I Climb

Rock climbing has been one of my favorite hobbies for several years now. What started as something I just did for fun when I was little turned into a great passion of mine. The combination of physical challenge, problem-solving, and the sheer exhilaration of reaching the top of a route always keeps me coming back. Whether I'm at an indoor gym working on technique or out on real rock soaking in the scenery, climbing never gets old.

If you've ever been curious about getting into rock climbing, this page is for you. I'll walk you through the basics: the different types of climbing, the gear you'll need, and a step-by-step approach to getting started safely.

Types of Rock Climbing

There are several disciplines within rock climbing, each with its own flavor and appeal:

  1. Bouldering — Short, powerful routes (called "problems") done without a rope, close to the ground with crash pads below.
    • Great for beginners because there are no rope systems to learn
    • Focuses on strength and technique
    • Indoor bouldering gyms are everywhere
    • Problems are rated V0 (easiest) to V17 (hardest)
  2. Top Rope Climbing — A rope is anchored at the top of the route. A belayer on the ground keeps slack out of the rope as you climb.
    • Very safe — falls are caught quickly
    • Ideal for learning movement on the wall
    • Common in indoor gyms for new climbers
  3. Lead Climbing — You clip the rope into protection as you climb upward, meaning a fall takes you below your last clip.
    • Sport climbing: bolts pre-drilled into the rock
    • Traditional (trad) climbing: you place your own gear
    • Requires more training and experience
    • Used in outdoor climbing and competitions
  4. Free Solo — Climbing without a rope at all. Not recommended for beginners.
    • Reserved for elite climbers with years of experience
    • Alex Honnold's free solo of El Capitan is the most famous example
    • Extremely dangerous — not a starting point
💡 My recommendation: Start with bouldering or top-rope at a local indoor gym. It's cheap, safe, and social. Most gyms offer a beginner orientation that covers everything you need to know.

Essential Gear to Get Started

You don't need much to start, especially at an indoor gym where you can rent almost everything. Here's what you'll eventually want to own:

Beginner Gear Checklist

  1. Climbing Shoes
    • Should fit snugly — your feet will power most of your movement
    • Beginner shoes: flat sole, comfortable fit (~$60–$90)
    • Advanced shoes: downturned, aggressive fit
    • Don't start with super tight performance shoes
  2. Chalk & Chalk Bag
    • Keeps hands dry for better grip
    • Small bag clips to your waist (~$15–$25)
    • Some gyms have chalk restrictions — check first
  3. Harness (for roped climbing)
    • Fits around waist and legs
    • Should be snug, not painful
    • Gyms often rent these (~$5/day)
  4. Belay Device & Locking Carabiner (for roped climbing)
    • ATC or GriGri are the most common devices
    • Requires a belay certification at most gyms
    • Take a belay class — it's easy and only takes an hour

How to Get Started — Step by Step

  1. Find a local gym — Search for bouldering or climbing gyms near you. Many cities have multiple options.
  2. Go on a weekday — Gyms are less crowded and staff have more time to help newcomers.
  3. Take a beginner orientation — Usually free or cheap, covers safety and gym rules.
  4. Start on easy routes — Look for V0 or 5.6–5.8 rated routes. Don't ego-check yourself into injury on day one.
  5. Climb with a friend — It's more fun, you'll learn from each other, and a belayer is required for top rope.
  6. Be consistent — Climbing 2–3x per week builds strength and technique much faster than occasional sessions.
  7. Work on footwork — Beginners rely too much on arms. Trust your feet early and your climbing will improve dramatically.
💡 Most people are sore in strange muscles their first week. That's normal. Rest days are part of training.
